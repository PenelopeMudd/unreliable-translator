On the Loss of a Friend

I have been fortunate enough to bask in the glow of three women who had a remarkable way of showing people they matter, my Aunt Betty, my Momma, and my friend Kathy Westhoff. This morning is Kathy’s funeral in DeSoto where the whole Westhoff clan will be to send her off with laughter and tears. In honor of the love she showered on those around her, I offer this.

Kathy came into my life pretty soon after I moved to DeSoto with my mom when my parents’ divorced in 1985. As freshmen in high school, we were up to the “usual things” (full wink intended) our peers were doing, circa 1980s Jefferson County. Most of us were just trying not to get in trouble and have our freedoms revoked. Kathy’s daughter Wendy and our group of friends had known each other since elementary, so I was a new addition to the troop of girls who trampled through Kathy and Dick’s house in the way that only teenagers can. For most of her career, Kathy worked at DeSoto High School as a secretary, making her a fixture in hundreds of DeSoto families’ lives.

For context, I feel like it’s important to discern the difference between Wendy’s home and mine in those days. This is not a critique of my mom or her choices. Mom was in survival mode most of our four years in DeSoto. After 38 years, Dad decided he did not want to be married anymore, so Mom bought a house in “town” and got started reassembling a life that she never chose to decimate. By cleaning houses and shrewd financial management, Dorris Wilson hoped to raise this last untamed Wilson girl to some sort of respectability and then to decide what to do with life as a single woman in her 50’s.

In contrast, Wendy’s house felt like what it’s “supposed to be” to me. There is no way that I’m not going to flatten this, but many of you were there in Kathy and Dick’s house over the years, so you will know. I can still hear Dick’s voice bellowing “Matthew!” from the kitchen. I have no idea what poor Matt, Wendy’s older brother, had done that day, but we girls had a front row seat in the living row. Wendy Louise will hate this — but I mean — she graduated 2nd in our class — she has always been brilliant, diligent, and motivated. Wendy is an achiever on her own, but her family’s love and expectations gave her a metric for what that success could look like for her, and they held her accountable in a way that can feel stifling as a teen, but makes so much more sense as a parent. Then there’s Jennifer — Jen — the only little sister I ever met who was never a pain. The five of them were a unit — which isn’t to say that there was never strife, but Kathy and Dick were a team and their children benefited from their love and unity, but so did countless other school, neighborhood, and occasionally feral children who wandered into their lives.

For many, this story would end with childhood. Graduations launch friends to junior colleges, universities, and careers that make it logistically improbable for many to maintain consistent connection, especially in the low-tech 90s. However, some time between the end of high school and her passing, Kathy moved from merely Wendy’s mom to Kim’s friend as well.

In 1990 when I was still a child making adult decisions, “in trouble” became more visceral as I found out that I was pregnant about 4 months before graduation. The previous fall, I completed an in-person interview in North Carolina for an all-women’s college and hoped to be accepted. In truth, I have always been an excellent test taker, but a horrible student, so I’m not sure how my non-stellar GPA was faring on the East Coast, but I can only assume they must have had different metrics back then or it was private and my family was willing to pay for college as they had offered to for my four older siblings. Alas, it was not to be as Jordan Louise was born in Minnesota on June 20, 1990, at 25½ weeks, 1lb, 11oz. There’s a lot of story in between there, but suffice it to say, by the time Jordan was around 6 months old, I was living back in the DeSoto area, a single mom and 18 years old.

Over the years, Kathy invited me to holidays. She watched my kids, including Brooke who arrived in 1992, and she smiled every time she saw me no matter what sort of disarray my life was in at the moment. Unlike many, Kathy never looked at me like I failed because I had made my life more complicated. I felt like of all the people in the world, she was one who at least saw that I was trying my best, no matter the outcomes. Over the years, she would show up to cheer me on when I graduated college and when I decided to pursue a PhD by hand-delivering the book Wonder for my study. For years, I carried the sense that there must have been something special Kathy saw in me for her to single me out so, and there were many times I used that knowledge to move forward when I wanted to give up.

It wasn’t until Kathy got sick that I began talking with others about her impact on my life. As friends traded stories, I discovered something I hadn’t known: Kathy had welcomed a collection of uncertain girls over the years. Each of us seemed to have carried our own private evidence that Kathy had singled us out somehow — that she had seen something in us worth noticing. Finding out this shared experience made me think, “Of course she did.” Kathy had somehow managed to make a whole collection of people feel individually seen, and none of us knew the scale of what she had been doing until we began telling one another. To us, through Kathy, the Westhoff family had this bonfire of belonging, and we were among a fortunate few welcomed to heal in its warmth.

I didn’t go see Kathy during her final six weeks. After my mom’s passing, I had learned that sometimes hospice is for the living as well as the dying. I didn’t believe my showing up would give Kathy anything she needed, and I knew she was clear on my love for her. So I stayed away. I don’t know whether that was the right decision, but I know she would understand.

While Kathy’s family celebrated her life, I did as well, sitting on my porch and writing this. I thought about Aunt Betty, Mom, and Kathy, and about what the world loses when people who have this remarkable way of showing others they matter leave our plane. I’m left with this: We honor them by saying their names and telling their stories while we reorient to the vacuum their losses have created. But one day, when we wake to a world that’s brighter than these days make it seem possible, we will remember together, again, how they made us feel, and then we can extend that welcome to someone who doesn’t know she deserves it — yet.

Thank you Kathy, Dick, and the Westhoff family for always laughing and crying with one wayward girl who wandered into its path and never left.

Kim Starkey · 2026
